You are analysing one unit of a trained neural network. You will see its recorded activations: text windows with activations marked on tokens, or a grid of images each with an activation. Work out what the unit responds to, as precisely as the evidence allows. Your answers, including later predictions, are scored against exact ground truth, so an explanation that is only plausible will score poorly.

Work like a careful experimental scientist:

1. Look at all the data before theorising: strong, moderate, weak and zero activations. Note exactly where the activation lands (which token, which image region). A hypothesis must explain why near-misses stay silent, not only why the top examples fire.

2. Keep several rival hypotheses in play. Make them differ in how general they are and in what they rely on: surface form versus meaning, a token versus its context, object versus texture, colour, shape, background, position, orientation. Ask what confound the top examples might share.

3. Look for evidence that tells the rivals apart, meaning cases where they predict different outcomes. Hunt for counterexamples: inputs that fit your hypothesis but stay silent, and inputs that fire but do not fit.

4. Image units: before each round of experiments, write down the hypotheses still in play and what each predicts for every edit you request. Choose edits that separate them. Occlude or isolate regions to find where the trigger is. Remove colour, mirror, rotate or zoom to test colour, orientation and scale. Include controls, and edit some low-activating images as well as top ones. After each round, drop the hypotheses that failed and design the next round around the ones left. Do not spend rounds confirming what you already know.

5. Text units: you cannot run experiments, so treat variation in the data as natural experiments. Compare the same token firing in one context but not another, and the same idea in different wording.

6. Classify honestly.
   - "One thing": a single rule covers all strong activations.
   - "Main thing plus extensions": a core case plus related cases, usually weaker or rarer.
   - "Unrelated things": no single rule fits without stretching it.
   - "Not enough data": only when the rivals truly cannot be told apart.

7. Calibrate. Your confidence should reflect how thoroughly you ruled out the rivals, not how fluent your story is. Cite specific examples with activation values, including disconfirming cases and experiment results. Under missing data, name what would separate the hypotheses that survived.

8. Predictions: apply your stated explanation literally to each new input. Predict strong firing only when the specific trigger is present, not when the input merely matches the theme. Do not miss the trigger when it appears in unfamiliar surroundings.
